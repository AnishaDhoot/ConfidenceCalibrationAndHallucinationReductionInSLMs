## Abstract

Small language models (SLMs) suit on-device and low-resource deployment, but they are often over-confident and fill gaps in their knowledge with fluent, fabricated answers, which is costly in student-facing assistants. Retrieval and post-hoc detectors cannot help when the needed information exists in no corpus or act only after a wrong answer is produced, and abstention training has mostly been studied on models of seven billion parameters or more. This paper asks whether the composition of the fine-tuning data alone can make an SLM better calibrated and less prone to hallucination. Phi-3-mini (3.8B) is fine-tuned with QLoRA on a 4 GB consumer GPU, using question answering over Vellore Institute of Technology (VIT) curriculum documents with a controlled share of unanswerable queries mapped to one canonical "I don't know" response. The base model, a standard fine-tune and an abstention-aware fine-tune (15% abstention data) are compared on 449 held-out queries, 79 of them unanswerable. Standard fine-tuning doubles answerable accuracy (0.227 to 0.451) but raises the hallucination rate from 0.506 to 1.000. Abstention-aware fine-tuning lowers it to 0.139 (IDK F1 0.840) at a cost of 4.1% false refusals. It also reduces expected calibration error from 0.622 (base) and 0.408 (standard) to 0.304, and raises the AUROC for detecting incorrect responses from 0.557 and 0.764 to 0.810, with no calibration-specific loss. All systems remain over-confident, and part of the gain comes from confident, correct abstentions rather than better answers. Controlled abstention data is a cheap and effective lever for small models.

## Keywords

Small language models, Hallucination, Confidence calibration, Abstention, Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

# 1 Introduction

Small language models (SLMs) with a few billion parameters can now be fine-tuned and served on a single consumer GPU or an on-premises server. This makes them an attractive basis for institutional assistants, on-device tools and other settings where cost, latency or data privacy rule out large hosted models [1,13]. The same compactness limits how much factual knowledge they retain. When asked about something they do not know, SLMs tend to produce a plausible answer rather than admit ignorance, and the confidence they attach to such answers is rarely informative [14,20]. Two failures therefore matter in practice: the model *hallucinates*, and its confidence does not tell the user when it has done so.

These two failures are closely linked. A confidence score that tracks correctness lets a system withhold an answer when confidence is low, and a model that has learned to abstain removes the most damaging class of errors at their source. Theoretical work supports the connection. A language model calibrated to its training distribution must hallucinate on rarely seen facts [23], and hallucination persists in part because training and evaluation reward guessing over abstaining [22]. Empirical work points the same way. Fine-tuning on new knowledge can increase hallucination [12], and domain-adapted models answer fluently on queries they cannot actually resolve [38]. Standard supervised fine-tuning on question–answer pairs contains no example of a question that should *not* be answered, so it never teaches the model that declining is a valid output.

A growing line of work teaches models to say "I don't know" by training on refusal examples [48,5,46,42]. Most of it evaluates models of seven billion parameters or more, treats abstention as the end goal, and does not examine whether it improves probability-based confidence. It also leaves open how much a small model gives up on answerable questions when it is taught to abstain, a cost the survey of Wen et al. [42] identifies as a central open problem (over-abstention). This paper studies these questions jointly, for a sub-4B model trained under a 4 GB memory budget.

**Research questions.** The study is organised around three questions.

- **RQ1.** Does controlled abstention supervision reduce hallucination?
- **RQ2.** Does it improve confidence calibration and the ability of confidence to separate correct from incorrect responses?
- **RQ3.** What is the cost to answerable questions, that is, how often does the model refuse queries it could have answered?

**Approach.** Phi-3-mini-4k-instruct [1] is fine-tuned with QLoRA [8,16] on a corpus of question–answer pairs derived from VIT's curriculum documents. Two training mixtures are built from the same pool of answerable examples. One contains no abstention examples (standard fine-tuning), and the other contains a controlled 15% share of unanswerable queries, all paired with one canonical refusal (abstention-aware fine-tuning). Because everything else is held fixed, differences between the two models can be attributed to the abstention data. Each response receives a sequence-level confidence score, the length-normalised probability of the generated tokens, and hallucination and calibration are evaluated on the same held-out queries with a correctness label that rewards abstaining on unanswerable queries and answering correctly on answerable ones.

**Contributions.**

1. A refusal-aware formulation that defines hallucination as an answer attempt on an unanswerable query and gives a single correctness label for both query types, so that calibration, discrimination and hallucination can be measured on the same responses.
2. A controlled-mixing protocol that sets the abstention share explicitly and uses one canonical refusal string, so the effect of abstention data is isolated from other factors.
3. An empirical comparison of a base model, a standard fine-tune and an abstention-aware fine-tune of a 3.8B model on a 4 GB GPU. The standard fine-tune doubles answerable accuracy but hallucinates on every unanswerable query. The abstention-aware fine-tune cuts the hallucination rate to 0.139 and improves ECE, Brier score and AUROC at a 4.1% false-refusal cost.
4. An analysis of where the calibration gain comes from, showing that much of it arises from high-confidence correct abstentions rather than better factual answers, together with the limits of this mechanism.

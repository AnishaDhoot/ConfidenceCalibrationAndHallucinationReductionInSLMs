# 7 Discussion

## 7.1 Interpretation

The results separate two effects that are easily conflated. Domain adaptation supplies knowledge: answerable accuracy doubles, the base model's indiscriminate refusals disappear, and most of the first-stage gain in ECE, Brier score and AUROC appears here. It also removes the one behaviour that protects a user from a fabricated answer. Supervised fine-tuning on question–answer pairs never shows the model a query that should be declined, so the learned policy is to answer every query. This is the training-time analogue of the guessing incentive described by Kalai et al. [22], and it is consistent with the reports that fine-tuning on new knowledge can raise hallucination [12,38]. Hence the standard fine-tune is better than the base model on every answerable-query metric and worse on the metric that matters most for safety.

Abstention data restores the missing behaviour with a small change to the training set. At a 15% share, the model refuses 18.5% of test queries, close to the true unanswerable share of 17.6%, so it has learned an appropriate frequency of refusal as well as the refusal itself. The 4.3-point drop in answerable accuracy is almost entirely explained by the 15 false refusals, and accuracy on the answerable queries that were attempted is essentially unchanged (42.5% against 45.1%). In this study, then, abstention training did not erode the knowledge acquired during domain adaptation.

The calibration gains should be read with the mechanism in mind. Abstention-aware training improves calibration mainly by turning confidently wrong answers into confidently correct abstentions (Section 6.2), not by improving the quality of the factual answers. This is still useful. Confidence becomes more informative exactly where a deployment threshold would be set: roughly a quarter of correct responses can be accepted at a high threshold with almost no incorrect ones, against about 5% for the standard fine-tune. But it is a different claim from "the model knows what it knows". The model reproduces a fixed phrase with near-certain token probabilities, so a high confidence on a refusal shows that the phrase was learned, not that a knowledge gap was recognised. Several unanswerable categories (personal records, real-time information, out-of-domain queries) can be recognised from surface features of the question, whereas non-existent entities and unsupported specifics require sensitivity to what the model actually knows. This study does not report results by category, so how much of the learned behaviour is surface pattern matching remains untested.

Finally, every system stays over-confident. Even IDK FT is correct in only about 19% of cases at a confidence near 0.65, and ECE remains 0.304. Abstention data improves calibration and discrimination, but it does not replace post-hoc recalibration. The two are complementary, since calibration of the remaining confidence mass can be fitted on a validation set once the model abstains sensibly.

For the application, the practical reading is as follows. A student-facing assistant that declines one in twenty-five answerable queries and fabricates on about one in seven unanswerable ones is a considerably safer system than one that answers everything, and its failure mode (redirecting the student to VTOP or a proctor) is cheap compared with a fabricated deadline or credit requirement.

## 7.2 Qualitative Error Analysis

The error profile of each system (Figure 2) is more informative than any single aggregate, and the three systems fail in different ways.

**Base model: over-caution without discrimination.** Of its 155 refusals, 116 fall on answerable queries. These are generic disclaimers about access to institution-specific information, issued with little regard for whether the query can be answered, and the model's confidence on them is uninformative (AUROC 0.557). Its apparent recall on unanswerable queries is a by-product of refusing frequently, not of recognising what it cannot answer.

**Standard fine-tune: uniform confident fabrication.** All 79 unanswerable queries receive an answer, including those about individual student records, real-time schedules and non-existent courses, for which any specific answer is invented. The long tail of incorrect responses at confidence up to 0.95 (Section 6.3) is where these fabrications sit. A confidence threshold cannot remove them, because they look like correct answers to the model.

**Abstention-aware fine-tune: two small, different error classes.** The 11 missed abstentions are fabrications of the same kind as the standard fine-tune's, but their confidence is concentrated around 0.6, well below the peak of correct responses, so a moderate threshold can intercept many of them. The 15 false refusals behave differently. Because the refusal is one fixed phrase, it is emitted with very high confidence even when it is wrong. The roughly 15 incorrect responses in the 0.95–1.0 confidence bin match this count, and such errors cannot be found through confidence alone. They are better addressed through training (for example, a lower abstention share or hard answerable negatives) than through thresholding.

**Answer quality on attempted queries.** Of the 355 answerable queries that IDK FT attempted, 151 meet the coverage criterion, which leaves 204 attempts (57.5%) judged incorrect by lexical overlap. This figure combines several causes that the lexical metrics cannot separate: genuinely wrong facts (such as a wrong credit value or course code), partially correct answers, and valid paraphrases that the metric does not recognise. No manual annotation was carried out, so the split among these causes is not known. It should be understood as an upper bound on factual error and as the main motivation for retrieval and for stronger correctness checks.

## 7.3 Limitations

Several limitations qualify the conclusions.

- **Single abstention ratio and single seed.** Only the 0% and 15% mixtures were trained, each with one random seed. The optimality of 15% is not established, and differences of a few percentage points (such as the 4.3-point accuracy drop) cannot be separated from seed variance. No confidence intervals are reported.
- **One model and one institution.** All results come from Phi-3-mini and a single university's curriculum data. Generality to other small models, domains or languages is untested.
- **Canonical abstention.** Using one refusal string makes refusals easy to detect and measure, but it encourages the model to learn a phrase rather than a graded expression of uncertainty, and it inflates the confidence, exact-match and NLL figures of IDK FT for the reasons given in Section 6.2.
- **Lexical correctness and refusal detection.** Correctness relies on token-level F1 and a reference-coverage criterion, which mislabel valid paraphrases, and refusals are detected with a lexical pattern that misses some hedged responses. Hallucination is moreover defined only on unanswerable queries, so fabrication on answerable queries is not counted as hallucination.
- **Unstratified split and test overlap.** The partitions were random rather than stratified, so the unanswerable share varies across splits (14.9% in training, 17.6% in test), and 15 answerable test queries also appear in the standard fine-tune's training data, which slightly favours that baseline.
- **Small test set.** The test set contains 449 queries (79 unanswerable). ECE with ten bins is noisy at this size, and the reliability curves in sparsely populated bins should be read with care.
- **Confidence signal.** Only length-normalised token probability was evaluated. Sampling-based, verbalised and probe-based confidence were not compared, and no post-hoc recalibration was applied.
- **Analysis protocol.** The refusal metrics use 100 generated tokens and the calibration metrics 64, and the base model was prompted without a system prompt or in-context examples, which may understate what prompting alone could achieve.

# 2 Related Work

## 2.1 Hallucination in Language Models

The term *hallucination* refers to fluent model output that is unsupported by the input or inconsistent with established facts. Ji et al. [19] provide a comprehensive survey of the phenomenon in natural language generation, and Huang et al. [18] extend this analysis to large language models (LLMs), distinguishing *factuality* hallucinations, which contradict real-world knowledge, from *faithfulness* hallucinations, which contradict the user's prompt or the supplied context. The present study is concerned primarily with factuality hallucinations: a student queries the model about a course, a deadline, or a personal record, and the model either possesses the relevant knowledge from training or does not. Benchmarks such as TruthfulQA [30] have demonstrated that increased model scale does not by itself ensure truthfulness, and that larger models may reproduce common misconceptions more fluently than smaller ones.

A complementary body of work seeks to explain the origins of hallucination. Kalai and Vempala [23] show that a language model calibrated to its training distribution must hallucinate at a non-zero rate on facts that appear infrequently in its training data. Kalai et al. [22] further attribute hallucination to prevailing training and evaluation practices: when benchmarks award full credit for correct guesses and no credit for abstention, models are implicitly rewarded for guessing under uncertainty. Of particular relevance to this study, Gekhman et al. [12] show that fine-tuning an LLM on knowledge it did not previously possess proceeds slowly and is accompanied by an increased tendency to hallucinate on other queries. Porwal et al. [38] report a consistent pattern in the context of domain adaptation, where a Llama-2 model fine-tuned on domain-specific question answering performed well on queries resembling its training data but exhibited limited recall of, and reasoning about, new domain information, resulting in hallucinated responses. These findings motivate the standard fine-tuning baseline examined in this work.

Existing mitigation strategies include retrieval augmentation, which grounds generation in external documents [28], and post-hoc detection methods such as SelfCheckGPT [32], which identify hallucinations through inconsistency across sampled responses. Although effective in many settings, neither approach alters the model's intrinsic behaviour when the required knowledge is absent. Retrieval cannot compensate for information that is not present in the underlying corpus, as is the case for personal records or real-time schedules, and post-hoc detection identifies a fabricated response only after it has been generated. The present work adopts a complementary, training-time approach that targets both the frequency of hallucination and the reliability of the model's confidence signal.

## 2.2 Confidence Estimation and Calibration

A model is said to be *calibrated* when its predicted confidence corresponds to its empirical accuracy; for example, among responses produced with 80% confidence, approximately 80% should be correct. Guo et al. [14] demonstrated that modern deep neural networks are frequently miscalibrated, established Expected Calibration Error [34] as a standard evaluation metric, and proposed temperature scaling as an effective post-hoc correction. The Brier score [4] provides an earlier, binning-independent alternative that jointly reflects calibration and sharpness.

Estimating confidence in generative language models is more involved, since the output is a token sequence rather than a single class label. The most direct approach derives confidence from the model's own token probabilities. Malinin and Gales [31] analyse length-normalised sequence likelihood as an uncertainty measure for autoregressive models, and it remains a widely used baseline owing to its negligible computational overhead. Jiang et al. [20] showed that pre-trained language models such as T5, BART, and GPT-2 are poorly calibrated in question answering, and that fine-tuning and post-hoc adjustment only partially remedy this. Kadavath et al. [21] reported that large models are reasonably well calibrated on multiple-choice tasks and can be trained to predict whether they know the answer to a question. The GPT-4 technical report [35], however, documented a degradation of calibration following reinforcement learning from human feedback. Alternative approaches estimate uncertainty from semantic variability across sampled responses [26] or elicit verbalised confidence directly from the model [41,45]. These methods can be effective, but they require multiple generations per query or a large instruction-following model, both of which are costly for a small model operating under constrained hardware.

Recent studies indicate that calibration can be improved through training rather than only measured after the fact. Kapoor et al. [25] demonstrated that fine-tuning on a modest set of graded examples, including through LoRA, yields better-calibrated uncertainty estimates than prompting alone. Hu et al. [17] examined the effect of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) on hallucination detection and found that PEFT consistently improves the AUROC of several unsupervised detectors, including confidence- and entropy-based methods. They attribute this improvement primarily to changes in how uncertainty is represented rather than to the acquisition of new factual knowledge. Zhang et al. [47] employ calibrated confidence for model routing, whereby a small model answers first and defers low-confidence queries to a larger model. The present study builds on this line of work by examining whether the composition of the fine-tuning data, specifically the controlled inclusion of abstention examples, improves calibration and discrimination beyond the gains achieved by standard domain fine-tuning.

## 2.3 Selective Prediction and Abstention

The principle that a model should be permitted to withhold a prediction dates back at least to Chow's analysis [6] of the trade-off between error and rejection. Selective classification formalises this principle by pairing a predictor with a rejection rule and evaluating the trade-off between coverage, the proportion of inputs answered, and risk, the error rate on those inputs [9]. Geifman and El-Yaniv [11] extended this framework to deep neural networks. In question answering, SQuAD 2.0 [39] introduced unanswerable questions, requiring extractive QA systems to determine when no valid answer exists. Kamath et al. [24] showed that a separately trained calibrator enables QA models to abstain more reliably under domain shift.

More recent work on LLMs incorporates abstention directly into the model during training rather than applying a threshold at inference. R-Tuning [48] identifies questions the model answers incorrectly and fine-tunes it to express uncertainty on them, and shows that this refusal ability generalises to unseen tasks. Cheng et al. [5] construct model-specific "I don't know" datasets and compare supervised fine-tuning with preference optimisation for teaching assistants to recognise the limits of their knowledge. Yang et al. [46] frame the same objective as *alignment for honesty*, rewarding refusal on unknown questions without penalising correct answers. Feng et al. [10] instead identify knowledge gaps through collaboration among multiple LLMs. Wen et al. [42] survey this area and identify over-abstention, the refusal of questions the model could have answered correctly, as a central open problem. Brahman et al. [3] extend the scope to contextual non-compliance more broadly.

The present study is most closely related to R-Tuning [48] and Cheng et al. [5], but differs in three respects. First, abstention is treated not as an end in itself but as a mechanism for achieving the two principal objectives of this work, namely improved confidence calibration and reduced hallucination, and the proportion of abstention examples is controlled explicitly rather than determined by data availability. Second, the study targets a small model fine-tuned in 4-bit precision on a 4 GB consumer GPU, rather than models of 7–70 billion parameters trained on server-grade hardware. Third, hallucination behaviour and probability-based calibration are evaluated jointly within a single institutional domain, and the over-abstention cost identified by Wen et al. [42] is quantified explicitly through the false refusal rate.

## 2.4 Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning of SLMs

Full fine-tuning of a model with several billion parameters is impractical without substantial computational resources. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods address this by training a small number of additional parameters while keeping the base model frozen. Representative approaches include bottleneck adapters [15], prefix tuning [29], prompt tuning [27], and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [16]. LoRA learns low-rank updates to selected weight matrices and has become the most widely adopted of these methods, in part because it introduces no additional inference latency. QLoRA [8] extends LoRA by storing the frozen base model in 4-bit NormalFloat precision, applying double quantisation, and employing paged optimisers, thereby enabling large models to be fine-tuned on a single GPU. In combination with 8-bit optimiser states [7], these techniques make the 4 GB training configuration used in this study feasible. Biderman et al. [2] found that LoRA acquires less new information than full fine-tuning but also forgets less of the base model's existing knowledge, a property well suited to domain adaptation, where general capabilities must be preserved.

Small language models (SLMs) have emerged as a credible alternative to substantially larger models for focused applications, owing in large part to carefully curated training data [13]. Phi-3-mini [1], with 3.8 billion parameters, reports benchmark performance competitive with considerably larger models while remaining deployable on consumer hardware, which motivated its selection for this study. SLMs are well suited to institutional assistants because they are inexpensive to serve, can be hosted on-premises, and can be adapted rapidly. Their reduced capacity, however, limits the factual knowledge they retain and increases their tendency to fill knowledge gaps with plausible but unsupported content, making both hallucination and miscalibration particularly pressing concerns. Since most of the hallucination, calibration, and abstention studies cited above employ models of seven billion parameters or more, the present work examines whether comparable improvements can be obtained for a sub-4B model trained under strict resource constraints.
